\begin{abstract}
Researchers often reuse one predicted item label across many records and
construct several aggregates from those records. With skewed item use, one mistake can
move several reported estimates together. A confusion matrix records how many
mistakes occurred but loses their locations.
This note asks what existing validation evidence can establish about estimates
built from such labels. For estimates linear in the labels, I give sharp bounds using
exact confusion counts and show when those counts identify the estimate.
A probability sample of already-verified target items can provide a simultaneous
count-based confidence certificate or a direct correction with a joint
design covariance for several estimates. In a browsing-data stress test, exact error
counts leave the signs of 84 group comparisons unresolved. Its user--domain
matrix also shows how record-level independence can misstate prediction-error
uncertainty even with independent domain errors. A separate
comparison of adult-domain classifiers shows similar information loss with
observed disagreements, without treating either classifier as truth. I also
distinguish these fixed-label results from score plug-ins, which need relevant
conditional mean assumptions, and from regression with aggregated predicted
covariates, whose coefficient is nonlinear in the labels. The practical task
is to match available validation evidence to the estimates and uncertainty
claims the researcher intends to report.
\end{abstract}

Suppose a researcher classifies reusable items, such as websites, merchants,
products, or documents, and links their labels to observational units. The
reported quantity might be a total, a difference in means, or a coefficient
from regressing an aggregate outcome on observed covariates. The same label
may enter many units and several reported statistics, and its
influence depends on the particular quantity being estimated. Accuracy and
the usual false-positive and false-negative rates count items; they do not
record which influential items were misclassified. Knowing the exact
confusion matrix for the target collection therefore need not determine even
the sign of a group comparison.

The distinction between classifying items well and estimating an aggregate
well is established. Work on quantification addresses class prevalence
\citep{hopkins2010,forman2008,vaz2019quantification}; cost quantification
already allows nonnegative item-specific weights \citep{forman2008}.
Design-based supervised learning corrects aggregates using probability samples
of verified labels \citep{egami2023}. Election and financial auditing use
random checks to control weighted error
\citep{stark2008audits,shekhar2023audits}. Machine-generated covariates,
including indices assembled from classifications, are studied in regression
\citep{fong2021predictions,battaglia2025generated,kluger2025imputed}.
Reuse of an uncertain item across records has the same common-shock covariance
structure familiar from shift-share designs \citep{adao2019}.
These results motivate a narrower question: for one declared estimate and a
fixed set of target items, what does a particular validation summary actually
establish? Reused labels and signed comparison weights make that question
especially consequential.

I answer it for an already assembled dataset, taking its predictions and
validation protocol as given. First, I map each linear estimate back to its
item weights and derive the smallest interval compatible with exact
target-item confusion counts. I characterize when those counts are sufficient.
Second, I show how known probabilities for already-verified items give a
simultaneous count certificate or a direct correction and joint covariance for
declared estimates. Third, I apply the calculations to a browsing-data stress
test and to observed disagreements among adult-domain classifiers. The latter
is an information exercise because neither classification is adjudicated
truth. Aggregated predicted covariates and calibrated scores are related uses
of the same labels, but interpreting a regression slope or a score plug-in
requires additional assumptions. The note offers a way to match validation
evidence to the downstream estimand; it does not introduce a new classifier
or a new regression estimator. Choosing which labels to acquire in the first
place is the focus of the companion \texttt{fewlab} project
\citep{sood2026fewlab}.

\section{Reusable labels and downstream targets}\label{sec:estimand}
Let $y_j\in\{0,1\}$ be the reference label of item $j$, for
$j=1,\ldots,k$, and let $q_j\in\{0,1\}$ be its prediction. An item can enter
many observational units, and a unit can contain many items. A known matrix
$C=(c_{ij})\in\mathbb R^{n\times k}$ records how item $j$ contributes to
unit $i$'s construct. Its
rows might represent people, households, places, or time periods; its columns
might represent websites, merchants, products, employers, or documents.
Entries can be counts, durations, expenditures, or fixed shares, according
to the construct. The complete-label and prediction-based unit vectors are
$T=Cy$ and $S=Cq$.

For declared fixed unit weights $a$, a linear target and its item weights are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:estimand}
 \theta=a^{\mathsf T}T=w^{\mathsf T}y
       =\sum_{j=1}^k w_jy_j,
 \qquad w=C^{\mathsf T}a.
\end{equation}
The weights can be nonnegative for a total or signed for a comparison.
Different analyses may use different matrices $C^{(r)}$ and unit weights
$a^{(r)}$, while reusing the same predictions $q$. Each then has item weights
$w^{(r)}=C^{(r)\mathsf T}a^{(r)}$. Fixed-denominator proportions fit this
setup by absorbing the known denominator into the rows of $C^{(r)}$.
If a denominator depends on unknown labels, or if the downstream statistic
is nonlinear, its target instead has the form $F(C^{(1)}y,\ldots,C^{(R)}y,Z)$
for other observed inputs $Z$; Section~\ref{sec:covariates} treats a
regression example.

A regression of a label-derived outcome on a full-rank, fully observed design
$X$ is still linear in the item labels. With
$A=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}$, its complete-label coefficient is
$\beta^*=ACy$. A coefficient contrast $r^{\mathsf T}\beta^*$ has unit weights
$a=A^{\mathsf T}r$ and item weights $w=C^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}r$.
These are descriptive coefficients for the specified frame; causal or
population interpretations require additional assumptions. Throughout, the
link matrices, unit weights, and fully observed regressors used to construct
them are fixed. Their uncertainty, if relevant, requires another sampling
model.

Browsing is one application. Here $c_{ij}$ can count visits by person $i$ to
website $j$. A difference between group means gives each person in group
$A$ weight $1/n_A$ and each in group $B$ weight $-1/n_B$. For browsing shares,
divide each row of $C$ by its observed total visits before applying
equation~\eqref{eq:estimand}. Replacing counts by duration changes the
exposure unit, and the label definition determines which content the result
measures; a domain-level label need not describe every page on a mixed site.

Let $e=q-y$. The prediction-based estimate is $\widehat\theta=w^{\mathsf T}q$,
so its error is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contrast}
 \Delta=\widehat\theta-\theta=w^{\mathsf T}e.
\end{equation}
Thus the estimand determines which classification mistakes matter. A widely
used item can dominate a unit's total but cancel from a group comparison if
both groups use it equally. A less common item used disproportionately by
one group can be influential for that comparison. An error rate that weights
all items equally need not describe either target.

The same prediction vector can enter several estimates at once. Let
$W=[w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(R)}]$ collect the item weights for $R$ declared
linear estimates. Their joint error is $W^{\mathsf T}e$, and under a
repeated-prediction model its covariance is $W^{\mathsf T}\Sigma W$.
The errors in two estimates share the same $e_j$ wherever both weights are
nonzero. A common item error may move two group means together and largely disappear from
their difference. Appendix~\ref{app:prediction} gives their joint covariance
under a repeated-prediction model. Retrospective correction from verified
items is given in Section~\ref{sec:audit}.

For fixed labels and a fixed fitted classifier, $\Delta$ is fixed.
If the question instead concerns repeated training and prediction, let
$b=\E[q-y]$ and $\Sigma=\Cov(q-y)$ under that process while holding $y,w$
fixed. Then $\E[\Delta]=w^{\mathsf T}b$ and
$\Var(\Delta)=w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w$. Reusing one uncertain item can make
errors in different units' aggregates covary even if errors across distinct
items are independent. Distinct item errors may also covary, but reuse alone
does not imply that they do. These moments require a model of repeated
prediction; the count bounds and probability audit below condition on the
realized predictions instead. Appendix~\ref{app:prediction}
gives the covariance details and a rare-error example showing why many items
alone do not justify a normal approximation.

\section{Implications for validation}
After data collection, retain the identity of each verified item and how it
was selected. For declared linear targets, known item inclusion probabilities
support a joint correction and design covariance even when the validation
allocation was inefficient. Exact confusion counts instead constrain the
number of mistakes but generally not their weighted sum; their sharp bounds
state what those counts alone establish. If several targets matter, their
different weight vectors determine which error locations the validation data
must preserve. Heavy item influence can make the covariance estimate unstable
and a normal interval unreliable even when the design formula is unbiased.

These fixed-label procedures need verified labels and, for an audit, a known
sampling design. They do not require calibrated scores. Applying error rates
from another collection requires transport assumptions; interpreting scores
as conditional probabilities requires stronger conditions described in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. For aggregated predicted covariates, estimating
their components does not by itself give valid inference for a regression
slope: numerator and denominator moments, their joint uncertainty, and a
nonvanishing denominator all matter. The browsing-specific common-rate correction and its assumptions
appear in Appendix~\ref{app:rates}.

\section{Bias and the denominators of error rates}\label{app:rates}
Let $\alpha_j$ be the probability of predicting one for a truly negative domain
and $\beta_j$ the probability of predicting zero for a truly positive domain,
conditional on the browsing design. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bias}
 b_j=\alpha_j(1-y_j)-\beta_j y_j,\qquad
 \E[d_i\mid C,y]=\sum_j c_{ij}\{\alpha_j(1-y_j)-\beta_j y_j\}.
\end{equation}
False-positive rates condition on true negatives; false-negative rates condition
on true positives. Multiplying a false-positive rate by predicted positives
uses the wrong denominator. Such a calculation would instead require the
probability of a true negative conditional on a positive prediction, together
with assumptions that justify transferring that probability to the weighted
population. These conditional probabilities are not interchangeable.

If $\alpha_j=\alpha$ on negative domains and $\beta_j=\beta$ on positive domains,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constantbias}
 \E[d_i\mid C,y]=\alpha(V_i-T_i)-\beta T_i
 =V_i\{\alpha-(\alpha+\beta)\pi_i\},\qquad \pi_i=T_i/V_i,
\end{equation}
where the share $\pi_i$ is defined for $V_i>0$. At a fixed true share, expected
bias scales with browsing volume unless the expression in braces is zero.
For $\alpha+\beta>0$, cancellation occurs at
$\pi_i=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$. Equal positive error rates cancel only when
half of visits are to truly positive domains. Thus the difference between
FPR and FNR alone does not determine bias. Nor does the number of distinct
visited domains: repeated visits matter through $c_{ij}$.

The common-rate assumption is stronger than knowing an average error rate on a
validation set of domains. Errors may depend on popularity, domain type, or
features associated with a particular group's browsing. In that case,
equation~\eqref{eq:bias}, with rates conditional on those features, is the
relevant expression. The bias in a share is $\E[d_i\mid C,y]/V_i$; dividing
by volume removes the mechanical scale factor, not misclassification bias.

With known $\alpha,\beta$ and $\alpha+\beta\ne1$, this model gives an unbiased
correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction}
 \widetilde T_i=\frac{\widehat T_i-\alpha V_i}{1-\alpha-\beta},\qquad
 \E[\widetilde T_i\mid C,y]=T_i.
\end{equation}
The correction is unstable when the denominator is close to zero and may return
values outside $[0,V_i]$. Clipping changes its expectation. Estimating error
rates introduces additional uncertainty shared across users; rates estimated
on unweighted domains need not transfer to weighted exposure. This is the
connection to classifier-based quantification \citep{vaz2019quantification},
with the additional requirement that the rates apply to each user's weights.


\section{A connection to label sampling}
The same mapping supplies a remedy when a researcher can verify a probability
sample of items. The companion \texttt{fewlab} project develops sampling
designs for reusable labels \citep{sood2026fewlab}. In its browsing application,
the regression decomposition is $H=AC$, with column $h_j$ giving the influence
of domain $j$ on the coefficient. For exposure shares, replace $C$ by its
row-normalized version. For any scalar target in equation~\eqref{eq:contrast},
the corresponding item influence is $w_j$.

Freeze predictions $q_j$ before drawing a label sample. Let $R_j$ indicate
selection, with known inclusion probability $s_j>0$ for every item. Conditional
on the fixed population and predictions,
the difference estimator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:difference}
 \widetilde\theta=\sum_j w_jq_j+
 \sum_j\frac{R_j}{s_j}w_j(y_j-q_j).
\end{equation}
Since $\E_R[R_j/s_j]=1$, its expectation over label sampling is
$\sum_jw_jy_j=\theta$, regardless of the classifier's errors. Verified labels
must measure $y_j$ correctly. Retraining the proxy on the same sampled labels
requires a separate argument; the fixed-proxy proof does not cover it.

Under independent Bernoulli item sampling,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:designvariance}
 \Var_R(\widetilde\theta)=
 \sum_j\frac{1-s_j}{s_j}w_j^2(y_j-q_j)^2.
\end{equation}
The realized sample gives an unbiased variance estimator by replacing each
summand with $R_j(1-s_j)w_j^2(y_j-q_j)^2/s_j^2$.
Fixed-size or balanced designs require their joint-inclusion terms or a
justified approximation. An inaccurate proxy can increase variance relative
to omitting it, but does not introduce design bias under these assumptions.
This is uncertainty from randomized label acquisition, with $y$ and $q$ fixed;
it is distinct from the prediction-error process in equation~\eqref{eq:moments}.
Both calculations preserve the reuse of an item label across downstream records.

\section{Auditing an aggregated regression covariate}\label{app:covariates}
The general plug-in error is $F(Cq,Z)-F(Cy,Z)$. If scalar $F$ is
differentiable throughout the segment from $Cy$ to $Cq$, it also has the
exact representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonlinearweights}
 F(Cq,Z)-F(Cy,Z)
 =\int_0^1
 \left[C^{\mathsf T}\nabla_TF\{Cy+sC(q-y),Z\}\right]^{\mathsf T}
 (q-y)\,ds.
\end{equation}
Unlike the fixed weights of a linear target, these effective item weights
depend on unknown labels along the path. Thresholds need not be
differentiable.

For the OLS coefficient in equation~\eqref{eq:truecovariate}, set
$d=C(q-y)$, $t=M_VCy$, $h=M_Vd$, and
$u=M_VY-\beta(y)t$, so $t^{\mathsf T}u=0$. If the predicted-regressor
denominator is positive, the exact error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariateerror}
 \beta(q)-\beta(y)
 =\frac{h^{\mathsf T}u-\beta(y)
              \{t^{\mathsf T}h+h^{\mathsf T}h\}}
             {(t+h)^{\mathsf T}(t+h)}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic term can remain when first-order terms cancel, and the
coefficient can move in either direction.

The conventional OLS score makes the two channels explicit. Write
$a=C^{\mathsf T}M_VY$, $G=C^{\mathsf T}M_VC$, and
$U(z;\beta)=z^{\mathsf T}a-\beta z^{\mathsf T}Gz$. For the fixed-sample
complete-label coefficient $\beta^*=\beta(y)$, $U(y;\beta^*)=0$, whereas
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariatescore}
 U(q;\beta^*)
 =e^{\mathsf T}(a-2\beta^*Gy)-\beta^*e^{\mathsf T}Ge,
 \qquad e=q-y.
\end{equation}
If repeated predictions are meaningful while $y,C,V,Y$ remain fixed, let
$b=\E[e]$ and $\Sigma=\Cov(e)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expectedscore}
 \E[U(q;\beta^*)]
 =b^{\mathsf T}(a-2\beta^*Gy)
  -\beta^*\{b^{\mathsf T}Gb+\operatorname{tr}(G\Sigma)\}.
\end{equation}
The trace term equals $\operatorname{tr}(M_VC\Sigma C^{\mathsf T})$.
With independent item errors it is
$\sum_jv_j\|M_VC_{\cdot j}\|^2$: each item's contribution depends on
variation in its unit-level loading after removing the controls. It is
nonnegative, but the other terms in equation~\eqref{eq:expectedscore} can
have either sign, and an expected score is not the expectation of the OLS
coefficient. In particular, ordinary attenuation does not follow. For
fixed binary $y$ and hard binary $q$, nontrivial errors cannot have $b=0$
item by item: a negative item can only have a positive error, and a positive
item can only have a negative one. Mean-zero error is thus not a harmless
default for this prediction process.

For several covariates built from the same predictions, let
$T_r=C^{(r)}y$ and $d_r=C^{(r)}e$ on the same regression rows. The
$(r,s)$ error cross-product in their residualized normal equations has
expectation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossnormal}
 \E[d_r^{\mathsf T}M_Vd_s]
 =b^{\mathsf T}C^{(r)\mathsf T}M_VC^{(s)}b
  +\operatorname{tr}\{C^{(r)\mathsf T}M_VC^{(s)}\Sigma\}.
\end{equation}
Even if distinct item errors are independent, the trace term contains
$\sum_jv_j(M_VC^{(r)}_{\cdot j})^{\mathsf T}
                  (M_VC^{(s)}_{\cdot j})$.
One item prediction can therefore distort the cross-product between two
constructed regressors. If the outcome also uses these predictions, its
measurement error contributes the analogous term
$\E[d_r^{\mathsf T}M_Vd_0]$ to the regression numerator, with $C^{(0)}$
building the outcome. Shared errors can then create an apparent association
as well as distort a regressor's denominator. These terms depend on how each
aggregation weights the same item; they need not produce scalar attenuation
of any coefficient.

More generally, let $D(y)$ collect $p$ columns of the form $C^{(r)}y$.
With an observed outcome $Y$, the
complete-label coefficient vector is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multicovariate}
 \boldsymbol\beta(y)=B(y)^{-1}m(y),\qquad
 m_r(y)=y^{\mathsf T}C^{(r)\mathsf T}M_VY,\qquad
 B_{rs}(y)=y^{\mathsf T}C^{(r)\mathsf T}M_VC^{(s)}y,
\end{equation}
when $B(y)$ is nonsingular. The same item label enters every component of
$m$ and $B$, so their audit uncertainty is joint. If $Y$ is also built from
these labels, its numerator moments are quadratic too. This is the usual
regression normal equation with its item-level construction made explicit;
it does not prescribe one particular correction method.

The fixed-sample coefficient in equation~\eqref{eq:truecovariate} has a linear
numerator and a quadratic denominator in the item labels. Put
$r=y-q$. Then
\begin{align}
 N&=y^{\mathsf T}a=q^{\mathsf T}a+r^{\mathsf T}a,\notag\\
 D&=y^{\mathsf T}Gy=q^{\mathsf T}Gq+2r^{\mathsf T}Gq+r^{\mathsf T}Gr.
 \label{eq:covariatemoments}
\end{align}
Let $R_j$ indicate an item's inclusion in a probability audit, with
$\pi_j=\Pr_R(R_j=1)>0$ and
$\pi_{j\ell}=\Pr_R(R_j=R_\ell=1)>0$ for every relevant pair. Set
$\pi_{jj}=\pi_j$. The following estimates are design unbiased for the two
moments, conditional on $C,V,Y,y,q$:
\begin{align}
 \widehat N&=q^{\mathsf T}a+
   \sum_j\frac{R_j}{\pi_j}r_ja_j,\notag\\
 \widehat D&=q^{\mathsf T}Gq+
   2\sum_j\frac{R_j}{\pi_j}r_j(Gq)_j+
   \sum_{j,\ell}\frac{R_jR_\ell}{\pi_{j\ell}}r_jG_{j\ell}r_\ell.
 \label{eq:covariateaudit}
\end{align}
The identities follow from $\E_R[R_j/\pi_j]=1$ and
$\E_R[R_jR_\ell/\pi_{j\ell}]=1$. No independent-item-error or calibrated-score
assumption is used. The ratio $\widehat N/\widehat D$ is generally not
design unbiased and can be unstable or undefined when the estimated
denominator is near zero or nonpositive. Valid inference for the ratio
requires additional design analysis; its variance involves inclusion
probabilities for sets of up to four items. The formulas identify the
information an item audit would have to preserve. The linear-label
difference estimator in equation~\eqref{eq:difference} does not by itself
correct a regression that uses $Cy$ as a covariate.

For a retrospective standard error, treat $(\widehat N,\widehat D)$ as a
joint vector of item- and item-pair Horvitz--Thompson totals. If the required
inclusion probabilities for sets of up to four items are known and positive,
its design covariance can be estimated from the realized audit. When $D$ is
bounded away from zero and a normal approximation for the joint moments is
justified, the delta-method variance for $N/D$ uses gradient
$(1/D,-N/D^2)^{\mathsf T}$. Near a zero denominator, a ratio confidence set
must instead respect the joint uncertainty in numerator and denominator;
the plug-in delta standard error is unreliable. With several constructed
regressors, the corresponding moment vector includes all residualized
regressor--outcome and regressor--regressor cross-products, including
equation~\eqref{eq:crossnormal}. Its covariance and the regression map must
be propagated jointly. These are routes to inference under an observed
probability-validation design, not a claim that an arbitrary validation set
supports an automatic corrected standard error.
